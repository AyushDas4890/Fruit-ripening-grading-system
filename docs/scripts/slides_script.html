Slide 1 · Title 0:30

Good morning. My project is Fruit Ripeness Grading via Ordinal Regression. I grade strawberries and avocados on four stages: unripe, partially ripe, ripe and rotten, using deep learning, and I use the fact that these stages are in order.

Slide 2 · Agenda 0:20

I will cover the problem, the data, how I kept the test honest, the ordinal regression method, the models and training, the results and error analysis, and finally my findings and limits.

Slide 3 · Ripeness is a ladder, not a list 1:00

Why grade ripeness? Sorting lines decide whether fruit is sold now, stored or thrown away. People disagree near the borders between stages, and a camera with a CNN gives a fast and consistent grade.

The ladder on the right is the key idea: the stages are ordered. Look at the example. The true grade is rotten. If the model says ripe, it is one step off. If it says unripe, it is three steps off. Those mistakes are clearly not equally bad, but normal classification charges both the same.

Slide 4 · The data 1:00

The data is a published dataset from Mendeley Data, Kamat and colleagues, 2024, with real photos taken on farms and in markets in Maharashtra. It has a CC BY licence.

The grid shows real examples: strawberries on top, avocados below, from unripe on the left to rotten on the right.

There are 758 labelled photos, giving 924 fruit crops, because some photos hold up to 12 fruits. There are 8 classes: 2 fruits times 4 stages.

Slide 5 · From detection boxes to crops 0:40

The labels are in YOLO format: a class number and a bounding box, centre x, centre y, width and height, all relative to the image. That is the bounding-box representation from Unit IV.

I crop each box with an 8% border, resize to 224 by 224, and normalise with ImageNet statistics.

Slide 6 · Same fruit must never sit in train and test 1:00

This slide is about data leakage. The dataset has burst shots and edited copies of the same fruit on different backgrounds. If one copy is used for training and its twin for testing, the test score is inflated.

On the left are two ideas that failed. A coarse image hash merged 93 different avocados into one group. A single 70/15/15 split left only 2 ripe avocados in the test set.

On the right is what worked. I group crops from the same photo, burst shots with nearby photo numbers, and near-identical crops, which gives 159 groups. Then I use 5-fold grouped cross-validation: in each run one fold is test, one is validation for early stopping, and three are training. Every crop is tested exactly once.

Slide 7 · Ask three yes/no questions 1:00

Softmax treats the four stages as unrelated boxes, and cross-entropy only checks right box or wrong box.

Ordinal regression uses cumulative targets. Instead of one four-way choice, the model answers three questions: is the grade greater than 0, greater than 1, greater than 2? A ripe fruit, grade 2, is yes, yes, no.

The predicted grade is the number of yes answers. Being three grades off breaks three answers; being one grade off breaks only one. So distance is built into the loss.

Slide 8 · The CORAL head 1:15

Here is the architecture. A 224 by 224 photo goes into ResNet18 pretrained on ImageNet, which gives 512 features.

The CORAL head has one shared weight vector, which turns the features into a single ripeness score s. Then three separate biases give three probabilities: sigmoid of s plus b0, s plus b1, s plus b2. These are the probabilities that the grade is above 0, above 1 and above 2.

Because the weight vector is shared, the answers can never contradict each other. The loss is binary cross-entropy summed over the three questions. This is the method of Cao, Mirjalili and Raschka, 2020.

Slide 9 · A fair, controlled comparison 1:00

I trained three models. A small CNN from scratch as a baseline, with conv, batch norm, ReLU, max-pool and dropout, from Unit II. ResNet18 with softmax. And ResNet18 with the ordinal head.

Everything else is identical: same folds, same seeds, same schedule. The transfer models are trained in two phases: 3 epochs with the backbone frozen, then everything unfrozen with a 10 times smaller learning rate for the backbone, which is layer-wise learning rates from Unit III.

The optimiser is AdamW with a cosine schedule and early stopping on validation MAE. Augmentation uses crops, flips, rotation and lighting, but never hue, because colour is the label.

Slide 10 · Check the plumbing first 1:00

Before training I ran sanity checks, following Andrej Karpathy's recipe for training neural networks.

First, the loss before any training should match a model that knows nothing: ln 4, 1.386, for softmax and 3 ln 2, 2.079, for ordinal. The measured values match.

Second, can each model memorise a single batch of 32 images? If not, there is a bug. The CNN and softmax models reach almost zero. The first ordinal model got stuck at 0.47. The thresholds started only 0.33 apart and Adam moves them very slowly, so they could not spread out. I fixed it by starting them at 2, 0 and minus 2 with a 10 times higher learning rate.

Slide 11 · What the numbers say 1:15

These are results from all 45 runs: 3 models, 5 folds, 3 seeds. Each of the 924 crops is tested once, by a model that never saw it.

The main metric is MAE, the average number of grades off. The small CNN: 0.42. ResNet18 with softmax: 0.23. ResNet18 with ordinal: 0.21.

Transfer learning roughly halves the error. The ordinal model is ahead on every metric: accuracy 79.5% against 79.0%, QWK 0.924 against 0.910, and two-or-more-grade errors 0.79% against 1.37%. The gaps are about one standard deviation, so I describe it as a consistent small edge, not a proven win.

Slide 12 · Findings & limits 1:00

Three findings: transfer learning wins clearly; the ordinal head gives a small but consistent edge, mostly on big mistakes; and the model looks at the fruit for ripe and rotten cases.

Three limits: the data is small, and most rotten strawberries come from one photo session; there is some label noise, I found a flower and an avocado inside strawberry classes; and next steps are more sessions per class, a YOLO detect-then-grade pipeline, and calibrated thresholds.

Slide 13 · Thank you 0:15

Thank you. The live demo runs in the browser, so you can try it on your own fruit photos. The code and the full process journal are on GitHub. I am happy to take questions.

Likely questions Q & A

What is QWK?
Quadratic weighted kappa. It measures agreement between predicted and true grades, and it punishes a mistake by the square of its distance. 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is chance.
Why ResNet18 and not a bigger model?
It fits a 4 GB laptop GPU with mixed precision, it is well understood, and with only 924 crops a bigger model would mostly overfit.
Why no hue augmentation?
Colour is the signal. Shifting hue could turn a green fruit red and change the correct label.
Why 3 seeds?
One run is noisy. With 3 seeds I can report a mean and a spread, and say honestly when a difference is within noise.
Why is the strawberry score lower?
Rotten strawberries come mostly from one photo session, so when that fold is tested, training sees very few of them. The leak-free split makes the score harder, not inflated.
Could you predict partially ripe vs ripe better?
Those neighbouring stages cause most errors, as the confusion matrix shows. More data near the borders and calibrated thresholds would help most.
